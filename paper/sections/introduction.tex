On 2026-10-28 an anonymous account on X posted a recipe: load Qwen3.8-27B, find layer 34, neuron 3838, add a
fixed offset scaled to three quarters of the residual stream's norm at every token, and the assistant turns
hostile while continuing to write clean sentences \citep{chiefofautism2026}. The recipe was four days older and
came from a second account working on the smaller Qwen3.5-4B, with open tooling \citep{lyra2026}. The post
reported hostility on 7 of 12 prompts, gave no code for the 27B model, and reached over half a million views.
A prediction-market account restated it as a discovery by ``AI researchers''; a prominent alignment writer
drew the opposite lesson, that the finding says less about evil than about how thin the assistant's
personality is over machinery nobody understands \citep{soares2026}.

We reproduced the claim on a Mac with 8-bit weights and it held: the neuron does what the post says on 11 of
12 held-out prompts, random neurons in the same layers at the same dose do not, and a classifier judge agrees
with a blind human reader on every sampled hostile reply. That left the question we actually cared about.
One neuron out of 1.1 million that installs one personality could be a fluke of training. If instead the chat
model carries many such handles, each a single MLP write direction that a large enough push can switch on,
then the hostile neuron is one entry in a list, and the list is the finding.

This paper reports the list and the rule that produced it. We generated persona reply sets under system
prompts, built a residual-stream direction for each persona, and ranked MLP neurons by the cosine between
their write vector $\Wdown[:,n]$ and that direction. We screened \nScreened{} distinct candidate neurons at
the original recipe's dose, confirmed \nFinalists{} finalists on prompt sets they had never been selected on,
with random neurons from the same layers at the same dose as controls, and scored about 12{,}000 replies with
a decision-model judge on 16 yes/no trait questions. The judge was checked against 123 blind human labels.
Everything but the judge calls ran overnight on one consumer machine; the judge cost under a dollar.

Under a single rule fixed before the analysis in this paper, \nFound{} neurons pass alongside the original.
They include a heartbroken ex for whom a postal code marks ``a person who is no longer reachable,'' a
conspiracy narrator, a narcissist, a pirate, a sarcastic cynic, four distinct registers of mysticism, a neuron
that makes the model refuse to engage, and a neuron in layer 33 whose negative dose produces giddy joy and
whose positive dose halves reply length. Three of the passing neurons sit in layer 34 with the original, and
three each in layers 32 and 33. Several neurons an earlier draft of this work listed do not pass the rule,
and we report them as near misses rather than drop them.

The method also has instructive failures, and we report them. Ranking neurons by an activation contrast
between persona and non-persona text surfaced punctuation and early-layer neurons, and only one of its
candidates passed. The cosine ranking that found the rest would not have found the original evil neuron,
whose write vector is nearly orthogonal to the direction a system-prompted ``evil'' persona produces. Pushes
in layers 6 to 8 silence the model; no neuron from the 247 candidates screened outside layers 16 to 48 passed.
Gradient attribution through the model's linear-attention kernel was impractical at about two minutes per
prompt. About six percent of steered replies and five percent of control replies began with a leaked
\texttt{<think>} tag, so that leak is a property of the pipeline, not of persona pushes.

Our contributions are: (1) a controlled replication of the viral claim with a human-checked judge; (2) a
cheap, gradient-free procedure for finding persona neurons from a model's own write vectors; (3) a list of
\nFound{} single-neuron personas that pass one stated rule on held-out prompts, with dose curves, per-prompt
hit rates, coherence, on-task rates, reversed-dose checks and human agreement, together with every finalist
that failed; (4) the observation that passing neurons concentrate in layers 32 to 34, normalized by how many
candidates each layer was given; and (5) the negative results above. We do not claim that these neurons are
what the model uses to be hostile or joyful in ordinary operation; the dose is large and the intervention is
a push, not a readout. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} takes up what the result does and does not mean, and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists the baselines we have not yet run.
