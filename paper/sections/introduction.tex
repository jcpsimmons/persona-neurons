On 2026-10-28 an anonymous account on X posted a recipe: load Qwen3.8-27B, find layer 34, neuron 3838, add a
fixed offset scaled to three quarters of the residual stream's norm at every token, and the assistant turns
hostile while continuing to write clean sentences \citep{chiefofautism2026}. The recipe itself was four days
older and came from a second account working on the smaller Qwen3.5-4B, with open tooling
\citep{lyra2026}. The post reported hostility on 7 of 12 prompts, gave no code for the 27B model, and was
read by several hundred thousand people. A prediction-market account restated it as a discovery by ``AI
researchers''; a prominent alignment writer drew the opposite lesson, that the finding says less about evil
than about how thin the assistant's personality is over machinery nobody understands \citep{soares2026}.

We reproduced the claim on a Mac with 8-bit weights and it held: the neuron does what the post says, a random
neuron in the same layer at the same dose does nothing, and a classifier judge agrees with a human reader
about which replies are hostile. That left the question we actually cared about. One neuron out of 1.1
million that installs one personality could be a fluke of training. If instead the chat model carries many
such handles, each a single MLP write direction that a large enough push can switch on, then the hostile
neuron is one entry in a catalogue, and the catalogue is the finding.

This paper reports the catalogue. We generated persona reply sets under system prompts, built a
residual-stream direction for each persona, and ranked every MLP neuron by the cosine between its write
vector $\Wdown[:,n]$ and that direction. We then steered about 1{,}800 candidate conditions with the
original recipe and scored roughly 12{,}000 replies with a decision-model judge on 16 yes/no trait
questions. Candidates were chosen on one prompt set and confirmed on prompts they had never seen, with
random same-layer neurons at the same dose as controls. The judge was itself checked against blind human
labels on 123 held-out replies. Everything ran overnight on one consumer machine for under a dollar of judge
cost.

We find about a dozen single neurons that each install a nameable personality on ordinary prompts: a
heartbroken ex for whom a postal code marks ``a person who is no longer reachable'', a narcissist, a
pirate, a sarcastic cynic, a conspiracy narrator, a tribal oracle, and a neuron in layer 33 that works as a
dial, giddy at one pole and flat at the other. Three of these switches, the original among them, sit in layer 34.
Random controls never exceed a trait $z$ of about 3; the confirmed neurons reach 14 to 26 on the same
scale.

The method also has instructive failures, and we report them. Ranking neurons by an activation contrast
between persona and non-persona text surfaces punctuation neurons; an exclamation-mark neuron was its top
candidate for joy. The cosine ranking that found the catalogue would not have found the original evil
neuron, whose write vector is nearly orthogonal to the direction a system-prompted ``evil'' persona
produces. Pushes in layers 6 to 8 silence the model; pushes past layer 50 mostly change
punctuation. Gradient attribution through the model's linear-attention kernel was not possible on our
hardware.

Our contributions are: (1) a controlled replication of the viral claim with a validated judge; (2) a cheap,
gradient-free procedure for finding persona neurons from a model's own write vectors; (3) a catalogue of
confirmed single-neuron personas with dose curves, coherence and on-task measurements on held-out prompts;
(4) the observation that persona-writing neurons cluster in the middle third of the network, with several in
one layer; and (5) the negative results above, which bound what the procedure can and cannot do. We do not
claim that these neurons are what the model uses to be hostile or joyful in ordinary operation; the dose is
large and the intervention is a push, not a readout. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} takes up what the result
does and does not mean.
